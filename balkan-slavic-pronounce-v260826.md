# Western South Slavic (North Balkan) Pronounciation Cheat Sheet for English Speakers - Version 2026-08-26

## Background:
### The larger South Slavic Group also includes Macedonian and Bulgarian, which is considerably different. Those two are part of the Eastern South Slavic (South Balkan) group.
### This Group contains: Slovenian, Serbo-Croatian; Bosnian, Montenegrin (Cyrillic script in Serbian is just a difference in symbols, the underlying language is exactly the same, very different from eg. Russian)
### If you see Serbian cyrillic text, you can simply use a transliterator to get out Serbo-Croatian in latin. A person from Croatia, Serbia, Bosnia and Montenegro would all sound almost the same if they spoke in a room, it would be nearly indistinguishable to an outside foreigner. An english person wouldn't normally be able to figure out who's from where. There's relatively small* differences and accents, etc.
### On average, Serbo-Croatian group has a harder time understanding Slovenian (Slovene)
### However, due to the fact that Slovenia never ethnically cleansed Serbs or anyone else from ex-YU out of the country after it's independence in 1991 (Ten Day War) and the fact that more immigrants from ex-YU kept arriving to Slovenia, and the fact that Slovenians are huge visitors to Croatian and Balkan summer vacation on the seaside, and the fact that Balkan (namely Croatian, Serbian, but not Bosnian-Muslim) music plays on a lot of radios and night clubs almost as per default, therefore, many Slovenians understand Serbo-Croatian pretty good for basic communication, and it's been said it's quite easy to learn, than vice-versa.
### The Slovenians of Serbian or ex-YU origin have been in Slovenia for quite a long time so they usually understand and speak Slovenian good if not perfect, their offspring may be fully bi-lingual.
### There is also a phenomenon in ex-YU Slovenian circles of switching back and forth and using Serbo-Croatian in slang or when upset, usually to make it sound more cooler or meaner/heavier. So when someone's upset and they blast a few bad Slovenian words, they would throw a few Serbo-Croatian bad words at the end for good measure. Native Slovenians probably don't do that. 

## Language functionality versus English:
### These languages are phonetically almost perfect, each letter usually maps to only one sound, and most letters are pronounced exactly as written.
### There are only 3 tenses: past, present, future. English "I saw" and "I have seen" is indistinguishable, it all just defaults to "past".
### There is a system of grammatical cases of nouns, pronouns and adjectives, called Declension, which means that a given root of the word can have many versions thorugh different suffixes, many more than english. 
### There is grammatical gender of nouns, feminine, masculine or neuter ("middle"), but on top of that, all verbs, pronouns and adjectives need to correspond to the gender of the noun in the sentence. 

## Because  You flip the words, adjust the suffixes and get to the same meaning.
This gives you an idea of the sheer amount of suffixes you need go get right for every word in every sentence. Suffix meaning differs greately between slavic languages too. Slovenian -a suffix may mean feminine, but in Croatian it could mean masculine.

Letters:

 **J is always pronounced as "Y"** - golden rule!
    - It also applies for almost all European languages except French, otherwise you will sound absolutely ridicolous and natives will probably be silently embarrased (but you will get polite correction) Older and/or less internationally up-to-speed native people may simply get confused because they genuinely wouldn't understand it. Your first sign that you're not doing it right should already be in the difficulty of saying these words with english J's, not to mention words with multiple J's. The European J is the English speaker's Joker!

C is always real C, never K, pronounced as "TS" - while K is just always K.

Č is (a shortcut for) "CH", - Ć is just a softer version of Č, slovenian doesn't have it, tourists don't need to worry about getting it right.

Š is (a shortcut for) "SH"

Ž is (a shortcut for) "ZH", (characteristically for english, Ž sound also appears in terms like "Garage", but there are other terms ending with "ge" with a different sound, read along)

Đ and DŽ is your "english J" sound - slovenian doesn't have it, serbo-croatian does, and it distingusihes between a hard and soft Đ. For some reason the hard one is DŽ and this is more accurately the direct equivalent to "english J". DŽ is a speciality in serbo-croatian along with LJ and NJ (on top of singular letters D L J N and Ž), these three have two symbols that are treated as one single alphabet letter making one sound.

G is always a hard G as in "Go!", never a soft one equivalent to "english J" like "George" (George ends with -ge, but differently pronounced versus Garage)

R is always a hard R, which is a trill/tap, not an approximant like in English, don't try to moan R's, say it like you're roaring like boar or bear

H is always pronounced, never silent, it is a raspy H such as in "loch ness" (this is where english context comes in, in this case the "ch" doesn't produce the Č sound, but what english describe as raspy H)

The other thing is, you're not suppose to collapse/reduce vowels at all, every letter is 99% a voiced sound almost the same way you see it written, with some very special rare exceptions in just several words. In practice, it's a bit harder, you have to forget every rule in english and just try to make every letter heard,

Slavic languages have declension (but so do other non-slavic ones, in different amount), you will see a lot of similar words with different suffixes, very complicated for english, don't worry about it unless you're trying to learn it.

This is just balkan south slavic, there's two other main slavic groups, they differ quite a bit still.
